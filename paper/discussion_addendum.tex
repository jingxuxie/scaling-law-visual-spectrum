\section{Implications for Optimizer-Aware Scaling}

The visible-spectrum viewpoint separates four quantities that are often bundled together as ``optimizer choice.''  Visibility $\theta$ controls the spectral power, damping $\rho$ controls the range where that power is active, first-moment momentum $\beta_1$ changes temporal constants and stability, and AdamW decay $\delta$ caps the active mode count.  Only the first quantity changes the local spectral exponent directly.

\paragraph{Component ablation.}
Table~\ref{tab:optimizer-components} confirms this separation in aligned stochastic training.  RMSProp, Adam, and AdamW learn almost identical $\qeff\approx1/2$, despite adding momentum and shrinkage, whereas SGD has $\qeff=0$.  Moderate finite-horizon decay therefore leaves the instantaneous spectral exponent intact; Theorem~\ref{thm:adamw} explains why fixed positive decay can nevertheless cap scaling asymptotically.

\paragraph{Forecasting and representation design.}
A pilot run with stable estimates $(\widehat a,\widehat b,\widehat q_{\rm eff})$ predicts effective exponent $\widehat\alpha=\widehat a(1-\widehat q_{\rm eff})$ and compute exponent $(\widehat b-1)/(\max\{\widehat\alpha,\widehat b\}+1)$.  Such a forecast should also report the fitting window, damping knee, and transformed-source diagnostic.  Visibility is jointly determined by optimizer and representation: whitening or global mixing can turn coordinatewise Adam into a scalar method without changing covariance eigenvalues, whereas band-aware groups or matrix preconditioners can restore spectral access.  In larger models, measuring $\qeff$ at several pilot scales tests whether the visible profile is stable; systematic drift predicts broken or piecewise scaling rather than a single global exponent.
